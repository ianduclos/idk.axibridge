# handover: one feature at a time (round 13, thread II-b) — 2026-10-18

**Outcome.** One line on an A4 sheet starts as Harold Cohen's 1969 plotter
meander (`meander69`) and ends as the fly from mosca-draw (`fly_open`). It
does not blend the two hands. It hands them over one feature at a time:
first the bends, then the handedness, then the corners. Each stop on the way
is a grammar of its own, which neither hand draws:

- the **bowed key**: B's bends between A's square corners;
- the **coiled key**: the same, but every bend and corner turns one way, so
  the cusps close into leaves and knots.

You can point at each stop and name it. The same hands, path and seed, run
as a blend or as cut-and-paste, show what this avoids (image 4).

## What I did

- **New voice `morph_handover`** (`kit/voices_handover.py`). Both hands grow
  their own token streams side by side, each by its own order-2 chain,
  untouched. Segment *i* of the line takes each of four features whole from
  stream A or stream B:
  - `corner`: whether there is a corner, and how sharp;
  - `bend`: the segment's kind and its turning angle;
  - `turn`: which way it turns;
  - `length`: its tempo.
- **No number is ever averaged.** Each feature has its own handover window
  along the path. Inside a window, a sticky two-state switch decides each
  segment; B's share rises from 0 to 1. So B's feature arrives first as a
  short run inside A, then in longer runs, and A relapses at the end.
- **The windows are staggered**, so the stretch between two windows holds a
  pure intermediate grammar.
- **Where it goes.** The line follows a path: boustrophedon rows, steered by
  K proposals per chunk. Progress is measured as the line's position
  projected onto the path, so each stage sits at a chosen place on the paper.
  Hand Drift's lines curled over themselves and hid their own morph.
- **Controls** in the same voice, used for image 4:
  - `mode: tokens`: whole segments of A or B, as Hand Drift's `mixture` did;
  - `mode: average`: every number moved from A toward B, as Hand Drift's
    `interp` did.
- **Why `turn` is a feature.** Measured on the training strokes:
  - `fly_open` keeps turning the same way at 0.97 of consecutive turns;
  - `meander69` does so at 0.54, which is chance.

  So the fly's loops come from handedness, not from curvature. That made
  handedness a feature of its own.
- **Pairs tried:**
  - `meander69` → `fly_open`, `trail_grazer`, `trail_oaknet` and `river_belt`;
  - `fly_open` → `meander69`;
  - `trail_grazer` → `meander69`;
  - `polygon_walk` → `river_oxbow`;
  - `polygon_walk` → `fly_open`;
  - `matisse` → `trail_borer`;
  - `molnar_letters` → `meander69` and → `fly_open`.

  Cohen → fly gave the clearest stages. `polygon_walk` → `fly_open` came
  second: its "arrowheads" stage is good too.
- **Frames tried:**
  - a 891 × 105 mm frieze: too thin to read;
  - 3 rows: too much empty paper;
  - 5 rows: reads as lines of handwriting, and the gestures are too small;
  - 4 rows on A4 landscape, 297 × 210: chosen.
- **Why A4 landscape.** It is the AxiDraw's full bed. Four rows give the line
  about 1.17 m of path, so every stage gets close to a row. The handovers fall
  inside rows, not at the U-turns. One continuous line, no pen lift.
- **Seeds used:**
  - survey and sweeps: 1311–1453;
  - the piece: seed **1442**.

## Duds that taught something

- **Tempo did nothing for this pair.** The `length` feature changes nothing
  between Cohen and the fly: their median segments are 0.056 R and 0.049 R.
  So the piece hands over three features, and `length` goes with the last one.
  With `trail_grazer` (0.34 R), tempo is the most visible change of all. Its
  long segments, though, burst out of any row (`orders-*` and `sheets1` in the
  out dir).
- **Wider windows** (0.18 instead of 0.10) give more foreshadowing events, but
  the stages muddy: key fragments relapse inside the bowed rows. I kept the
  narrow windows.
- **Corners first** gives a dead straight stage: Cohen's runs have no
  curvature, so without his corners only straight lines are left.

## Honest read

- **The axis II test passes.** You can point at:
  - where each change starts (the rings);
  - which way it runs (into the fly);
  - the gesture type before (Cohen's right-angle key) and after (the fly's
    loops).

  The two middles are their own things, not averages. The coiled-leaf stage
  in row 3 is the strongest, and it is the most sign-like.
- **Weaknesses:**
  - The bend handover is abrupt: its first event and its last relapse fall at
    the same place, 0.32 of the path. Only turn (0.54 → 0.58) and corner
    (0.77 → 0.83) show the "events, then runs" process.
  - At arm's length the bowed and coiled stages are close: both are full of
    cusps.
  - Read as a page, the boustrophedon can suggest lines of writing.
- **Would Ian keep it?** I think he keeps the idea and the specimen plate.
  Image 3 is "the drawing beside its measurement" again, and it opens a map
  of in-between grammars for any pair of hands. The piece may read to him as
  a strong process strip rather than a finished drawing. It is careful, and it
  lacks the improvised awareness of found ground. The obvious next step is to
  let what the line meets choose which feature is handed over next (axis I as
  the cause).
