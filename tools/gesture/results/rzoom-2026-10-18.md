# rzoom: the Amazon river zooming in and out along itself (round 14, thread c1)

**Outcome.** The far Amazon stem now zooms along its own length. It is still one contiguous line made of the real river's bends. The dense far twists open into large loops and close up again. The mechanism that works is variable magnification: re-integrate the stem's heading with paper speed ds = c·z·du. It is a kit voice, `river_zoom`, with a renderer, `rzoom_ink`, that weights the pen by zoom. The literal camera window (mechanism 2) is a dud. Sampling is not the risk I expected. The real limit is that the far data has no detail finer than 1.5 channel widths, so the top of the zoom range draws smooth balloons.

## What I built

- `kit/voices_rzoom.py`: the voice `river_zoom`, plus pure functions `magnify`, `zoom_shape`, `pace_warp`, `window_frames`, `heading_profile` and `shrink_fit`.
  - **magnify.** θ(u) is the frozen stem's heading (`river_stems/grwl_far_sb19`, 9684 points). It is re-integrated with ds = c·z·du, so curvature scales by 1/z. Heading is interpolated between stem segments, so the line has no corners at any zoom.
  - **Schedule.** z = zoom^g(τ). There are 9 shapes: push, pulses, drift, dive, steps, ramp_in, ramp_out, and two symmetric controls, swell and breath. The schedule is written over the drawn line: the camera moves while the river is drawn.
  - **pace** (0 to 1) re-times the schedule. At 0 the zoom is keyed to pen time. At 1 it is keyed to the river: the camera flies along it at constant ground speed. This turned out to be the main density control.
  - **Budget.** The whole stem is only 547 km of river. At the far scale (0.8 mm/km, which is Ian's found river) that is about 440 mm of paper. So the far view is expensive and zoomed stretches are cheap. The drawn length follows from the river and the schedule: 1.3 to 2.4 m per line.
  - **Direction of travel: steered, by default.**
    - The meanders (θ minus its 100-channel-width low-pass) are kept exactly.
    - A course heading carries the valley. It follows the river's own valley turns.
    - When a look-ahead point would leave the frame, the course turns toward a random point in the middle of the sheet. It turns no tighter than 45 mm radius, and holds the turn until it heads there.
    - The line is drawn at an absolute far scale and is only ever shrunk to fit, never clipped. So there are no stubs at the edge.
    - Why steer: free integration (`course="free"`) keeps every real heading, but the zoomed stretches shoot off in their local direction. Fitting that to a sheet shrinks the far twists to nothing (c fell to 0.14 to 0.34 mm/km). My first steering rule only pushed the line back from the walls, so it glided along them and left the middle empty. The commit-to-target rule fixed that.
  - **Channels:** `zoom` and `u_km` (where along the stem each point is), so thread b can place by zoom or by river position.
- `kit/renderers_rzoom.py`: `rzoom_ink`. It sets pen width per zoom band, with optional Flexoki ink per band. Runs are contiguous and share their end points, and short runs are merged.
- `experiments/rzoom_explore.py` (working sheets) and `experiments/rzoom_review.py`, which rebuilds the five review images.
- `tests/test_kit_rzoom.py` (20 tests). They cover:
  - constant zoom is a pure magnification (same turning function as the stem);
  - ds = c·z·du;
  - every shape visits both far and close;
  - steer stays inside the box, with a shrink-only fit;
  - the piece is one line with no jumps;
  - the band runs connect;
  - determinism across processes.

   - I did not make it a single line. A moving window is a sequence of frames, and joining them needs bridges the river does not contain. If the camera follows the pen, the line collapses into a scribble ball around the centre. Local magnification is the continuous form of the same idea.
   - The overlay reads as a diagram of zooming: frames start and stop mid-sheet, and they converge on the camera's centre point. The frames are also shortened to fit the box, so their ends sit in the middle of the sheet.

## What happened, in short

- **Sampling: not the problem.** Even naive magnification is smooth up to 66×.
- **Detail: the problem.** Above about 16×, the far data shows its absence of detail. A real river up close has bars, scrolls and uneven banks; the far stem has smooth lobes. At zoom 12 to 16 the bends keep their character (elbows, unequal lobes). At 24 to 32 they become balloons. The piece's loops are true to the data and still read as generic smooth loops. This is the handover thread c2's job (closer river hands). It is where `river_zoom` could hand off.
- **Symmetric vs asymmetric.** Swell reads as a closed figure: tapered at both ends, swollen in the middle. That is the ornament risk, and it is mild, because the river's own irregularity breaks the symmetry up. Push reads as events: something happens, then gets cut. Breath is less patterned than I expected, for the same reason. Under steering, symmetry hardly reads at all, because the tangle hides it.
- **Pen.** Banding by colour (blue far, black near) makes two lines meet. Banding by weight in one ink makes one line come nearer. So the piece uses weight. The colour version is one recipe change away and ties to the found-river blue.

## My honest read

- **The zoom does what Ian described.** You see small dense twists grow and shrink along one real river. The scale contrast inside one line is strong: about 1.6 mm bends against 80 mm loops. That is dynamics in the sense the round asks for.
- **As a finished sheet, it is a single line.** Ian ranked dense sheets above clean single relations in round 13. This line is moderately dense at its frame, not crowded.
- **The large loops are the weak part.** They are too smooth to hold up the space they occupy.
- **I think Ian keeps the mechanism, and maybe not this sheet as a piece.** It is most useful as found matter for thread b: one zooming river among other regimes, or two or three takes of the same stem at different schedules on one sheet.
- **For a stronger sheet,** lower the top zoom to about 16, or hand off to c2 above it.
